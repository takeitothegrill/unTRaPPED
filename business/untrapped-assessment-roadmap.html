The Purpose

Not a compliance audit. A community truth-telling tool.

There is a specific and common frustration in the wheelchair community: venues declare themselves "accessible" — and then reality disappoints. The ramp exists but the toilet doesn't work for a large chair. The "accessible" entrance is around the back, through a carpark. The bar is too high for a bartender to make eye contact. The dance floor is technically reachable but too crowded to use.

The unTR@PPED Field Assessment Tool exists to fix this. It gives community members — people with lived experience, support workers, and family members — a structured way to assess venues on behalf of the wheelchair community, and to contribute that knowledge to a shared, trustworthy dataset.

"
I don't need to be an occupational therapist or a trained building inspector — because the AI will guide me so that I know exactly what data points to capture, how to measure them, and how to document them."
— The unTR@PPED Future Vision

This is the destination. Getting there requires building a solid foundation first — and that is what this roadmap describes.

Core Design Principle

The outing as a journey — not a label

The assessment tool does not try to audit a whole venue. It follows the route a wheelchair user actually needs to complete — from arrival to doing the thing they came to do.

Every assessment follows this journey. The specific questions asked at each stage depend on the venue type.

The "primary purpose of visit" stage is what makes this tool genuinely useful — and genuinely different. It asks venue-specific questions rather than generic ones. What that means in practice:

🍽️

Restaurant

  • Can a wheelchair fit at a table?
  • Without blocking an aisle or other guests?
  • Is the table height usable?
  • Can menus be reached?
🍸

Bar / Nightclub

  • Is the bar counter reachable?
  • Can staff see a seated user?
  • Is the dance floor accessible?
  • Is there seating at usable height?
🛍️

Retail / Shopping

  • Aisle widths for manoeuvring?
  • Counter height at checkout?
  • Fitting room access?
  • EFTPOS terminal reachable?
🏥

Medical / Services

  • Reception desk reachable?
  • Waiting room space to park?
  • Consultation room access?
  • Equipment at usable height?
⚠️

Important boundary

The assessment tool is not a whole-venue audit, not an Australian Standards compliance check, and not a professional certification. It is a structured, community-sourced record of what was observed — on a specific route, on a specific day, by a specific assessor.

The Community Assessor

Lived experience is the credential

The unTR@PPED assessment tool is designed to be used by everyday community members — not professionals. The people most likely to use it are those who understand, at a personal level, what a failed outing means.

♿

Wheelchair users

Contributing to a shared resource for their community. Assessing venues they use regularly, or ones they are visiting for the first time.

👨‍👩‍👧

Family members

Scoping a venue in advance for a loved one — and choosing to share that information so others don't have to do the same work.

🤝

Support workers

Checking whether a venue is genuinely accessible for a specific client — and capturing that knowledge formally for future reference.

🏘️

Community advocates

People who want to improve access in their local area by contributing structured, trustworthy, reusable data.

💡

Design principle — respect, not instruction

Any onboarding or guidance built into the tool must be sensitive to the fact that many assessors will have extensive lived experience with wheelchair access. The tool guides the process — not the person. It assumes competence, not ignorance.

The Critical Requirement

Community data is only useful if it's consistent

The Common Data Standard

The long-term value of unTR@PPED depends entirely on one thing: every assessment, conducted by any volunteer anywhere, must capture data in a consistent, comparable format.

A measurement taken one way by one person and a different way by another person is not useful data — it is noise. For the dataset to inform other wheelchair users reliably, the tool must guide assessors to measure the same things in the same way every time.

This is the role the AI agent will eventually carry. In the short and medium term, it is the role the form design and field template must establish.

📏

Observed vs. measured vs. unknown

The assessment tool enforces a strict data discipline: Observed directly can become data. Not observed, not measured, or uncertain = null. Notes are allowed, but notes do not drive the assessment result. This prevents vague impressions from being recorded as facts.

Development Roadmap

Four phases. One destination.

The assessment tool evolves from a professional paper form through to a fully AI-guided digital platform — building rigour and accessibility at every stage.

1
Now
A Professional Field Assessment Form
Short Term

The immediate priority is turning the existing field notes protocol into a clear, professionally designed assessment form — something an assessor would be proud to carry and use, and that a venue manager would take seriously upon receiving a completed copy.

The form establishes the data schema that all future phases will build upon. Every field name, every measurement protocol, every null-handling rule defined here carries forward permanently.

  • Professional print-ready form layout with clear sections and logical flow
  • Structured fields for each route stage: parking → path → entrance → interior → toilet → purpose → return
  • Venue-type selector that surfaces the right "primary purpose" questions
  • Explicit observed / measured / unknown / null distinction baked into every measurement field
  • Assessor information section and date/time/weather capture
  • Photo reference log (photo numbers linked to specific observations)
  • Clear disclaimer section — what this assessment is and is not
The data structure established in Phase 1 is the foundation of the entire platform. It is worth spending time getting it right.
2
Next
An HTML Web Form — No Pen, No Paper
Medium Term

The paper form is converted to a mobile-first HTML web form that assessors complete on-site using their phone. No paper. No transcription. No lost clipboards.

The form is designed for outdoor and indoor use — large tap targets, high contrast, and a clear section-by-section flow. Assessors move through the form as they move through the venue, stage by stage.

  • Mobile-first responsive web form matching the Phase 1 paper schema exactly
  • Progressive disclosure — questions revealed as the assessor advances through each route stage
  • Venue type selection dynamically surfaces relevant "primary purpose" questions
  • In-form guidance: short plain-language prompts explaining what to measure and how
  • Photo capture integration — attach photos to specific observations directly from camera
  • Save-and-resume capability for longer or more complex venues
  • Completed assessment exportable as PDF summary for record-keeping and sharing
The HTML form should feel like having a knowledgeable colleague walking beside you — prompting, not instructing. The guidance tone must be respectful of lived experience.
3
Long
Term
Standards-Aligned Assessment Criteria
Long Term

The assessment criteria are built out against a three-tier regulatory framework — embedding the relevant standards directly into the questions asked, the measurements required, and the thresholds used to determine results.

This phase transforms the tool from "what we noticed" to "what the standards say this should be" — giving the community data genuine authority without overstating its legal status.

Federal

Australian Building Standards

AS 1428 (Design for Access and Mobility) defines minimum dimensions, gradients, surfaces, signage, and amenities for wheelchair access in new and renovated buildings.

State

Queensland Requirements

Queensland Building and Construction Commission (QBCC) requirements and any relevant Disability Services Queensland guidelines that supplement federal standards.

Local

Council Bylaws

Rockhampton and Livingstone Council planning requirements, footpath and public space standards, and any access-specific local provisions.

  • Each measurement field linked to its relevant standard and the minimum compliant value
  • Assessment results contextualised against standards — not just "measured X" but "X vs. minimum required Y"
  • Non-compliance flagging with plain-language explanation (not legal language)
  • Standards version tracking — so assessments record which version of a standard applied at the time
  • Venue-type specific standard subsets — restaurants reference different sections than nightclubs
This phase requires engagement with an accessibility consultant or occupational therapist to map the standards correctly. The tool informs — it does not provide legal advice, and should always make this clear.
4
Future
The AI-Guided Assessment Agent
Future Vision

The AI agent is the destination. It transforms the assessment tool from a structured form into an intelligent guide that carries the expertise — so the assessor brings the access and the presence, and the agent brings the knowledge.

The agent knows what kind of venue the assessor is standing in. It knows what data points matter for that venue type. It knows how to measure them. It knows what the standards say. And it guides the assessor through capturing exactly the right information — step by step, in plain language, without requiring any prior training in accessibility standards or building codes.

  • Venue-type detection or selection triggers a bespoke assessment pathway
  • Conversational guidance — "Now measure the door opening width. Hold your tape measure across the widest clear opening…"
  • Context-aware questions — asks follow-up questions based on previous answers
  • Real-time standard comparison — "That's 750mm. The minimum required is 850mm. Record it as non-compliant."
  • Photo guidance — "Take a photo of the threshold from this angle so measurements are verifiable later"
  • Uncertainty handling — helps assessors distinguish between what they observed and what they assumed
  • Completed assessment reviewed by agent before submission — flags gaps, inconsistencies, or nulls that need revisiting
The agent replaces the need for the assessor to be an expert. But it does not replace experts themselves. Occupational therapists, building inspectors, and lawyers remain the source of the rules the agent applies.
